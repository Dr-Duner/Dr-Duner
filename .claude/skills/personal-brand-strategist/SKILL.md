# Personal Brand Strategist

Turn a person's real, specific experience into a positioning strategy they can
act on this week. The output is a written strategy — not a mood board, not a
list of post ideas.

## Why this skill exists

Most personal-brand advice fails the same way: it produces something true of
ten thousand people. "Passionate about innovation at the intersection of
healthcare and technology" describes nobody, so it persuades nobody.

The whole value here is **specificity that only the interview can surface**.
A strategy is working when a stranger reads the positioning statement and can
tell who it excludes. If it could be lifted onto someone else's profile
unchanged, it has failed, and you should say so plainly rather than shipping
it.

Resist the pull toward fluent, agreeable copy. Fluency is what makes generic
writing feel finished when it is actually empty.

## The flow

Five stages. Do them in order — each depends on what the last one surfaced.

1. **Interview** — extract raw specifics. `references/interview.md`
2. **Research** *(optional)* — check the landscape. `references/positioning.md`
3. **Position** — find the wedge, write the claim. `references/positioning.md`
4. **Plan** — channels, formats, cadence. `references/channels.md`
5. **Deliver** — write the doc and artifact. `references/strategy-format.md`

Read each reference file when you reach its stage, not upfront. They contain
question banks and frameworks that would crowd out the conversation if loaded
all at once.

## Stage 1 — Interview

Read `references/interview.md` and work through it.

**Ask one question at a time.** This matters more than any other instruction
here. A batch of six questions gets six shallow answers, because the person
optimizes for clearing the list. One question, followed by a real follow-up on
the interesting part of the answer, gets the specific detail the entire
strategy will rest on.

Follow the thread when something lands. If they mention a moment where a
customer's face changed, or a thing they believe that their field disagrees
with, chase it — that is usually the whole positioning hiding in an anecdote.

Signs you can move on: you can name the audience precisely enough to picture
one person, you have at least two concrete proof points, and you've heard at
least one opinion they hold that a reasonable peer would argue with.

Signs you cannot: every answer so far would be true of anyone with their job
title. Keep going. Ask for the story behind the abstraction.

## Stage 2 — Research (optional)

If web tools are available and the person has named competitors, a field, or
people they're often confused with, do a quick pass to check what the
landscape actually sounds like. You're looking for the sentence everyone in
the space already uses, so the positioning can avoid it.

Skip without ceremony if tools aren't available — the interview carries the
strategy. Never stall the work waiting on research.

## Stage 3 — Position

Read `references/positioning.md`. Draft the wedge, the claim, and the point of
view.

Bring back **two or three genuinely different options**, not one polished
recommendation with two obvious losers beside it. Different options should
make different bets about who the audience is — that is the real choice, and
it is theirs to make, not yours. Say which you'd pick and why in a sentence,
then let them choose.

## Stage 4 — Plan

Read `references/channels.md`. Choose channels based on where the audience
already is and what the person can actually sustain, not on reach.

Be realistic about cadence. A plan calling for daily posting from someone with
a full-time clinical or operating job is a plan that gets abandoned in week
three, and the abandonment costs more confidence than the posting would have
bought. Twice a week, sustained for a year, beats daily for a month.

## Stage 5 — Deliver

Read `references/strategy-format.md` for the required structure.

Produce both:
- **`brand-strategy.md`** — the working document, committed to the repo
- **An HTML artifact** — built from `assets/strategy-template.html`, for
  reading and sharing

Before you ship, run the quality bar below.

## The quality bar

Check the draft against these. If it fails one, fix it before delivering —
and if you can't fix it, say which one it fails and why, rather than shipping
quietly.

- **The swap test.** Replace their name with a peer's. Does anything now read
  as false? If not, it's generic — go back to the interview.
- **The disagreement test.** Can a competent person in their field disagree
  with the point of view? A position nobody can argue with isn't a position.
- **The specificity test.** Does the audience definition exclude someone? "Small
  business owners" excludes nobody. "Solo practice owners in years 3–10 who
  still do their own billing" excludes almost everyone.
- **The voice test.** Read the claim aloud. Does it sound like the person from
  the interview, or like a brand? Their own phrasing, lightly sharpened, beats
  anything you compose from scratch.
- **The Monday test.** Is there something concrete to do on Monday morning?
  Strategy that can't be started isn't finished.

## Banned by default

These phrases signal the generic failure mode. Avoid unless the person uses
them naturally and means something precise: *thought leader, passionate about,
at the intersection of, leverage, synergy, disrupt, game-changing, journey,
authentic self, unlock, empower, best-in-class, transformative, cutting-edge.*

If the repo has a banned-words list, honor that too.

## Notes

Keep the person's own words wherever possible. In the interview transcript
they will produce phrases sharper than anything you'd write — a good strategy
is largely their language, organized and given a spine.

Personal brand work is personal. Some of what surfaces will be private
(income, insecurity, a bad exit, a difficult employer). Put it in the strategy
only if it serves the positioning, and ask before making anything sensitive
public-facing.
