# Positioning

Turn interview material into a defensible position. This is the analytical
core — everything downstream is execution.

## The wedge

A wedge is the narrowest claim that is both **true of them** and **useful to
the audience**. Narrow is the point. Broad positioning competes with everyone;
narrow positioning competes with almost no one, and can widen later once it's
established.

Find it at the intersection of three things:

1. **What they uniquely know** — from doing the work, not reading about it
2. **What the audience urgently needs** — a problem with cost attached
3. **What nobody credible is already saying** — the open lane

Where all three overlap is the wedge. Two out of three is a trap:

- Unique + needed but **already occupied** → you're fighting an incumbent with
  better distribution. Find the sub-angle they're too big to serve.
- Unique + open but **not needed** → interesting to them, invisible to
  everyone else. This is the most common failure among smart people.
- Needed + open but **not unique** → they'll be out-executed by whoever has
  more time, because there's no defensible reason it should be them.

### Testing a candidate wedge

- Could a competent peer say this exact thing with a straight face? If yes, not
  unique enough.
- Would the audience pay, subscribe, or forward for this? If no, not needed.
- Search it. If page one is saturated with established voices, go narrower.

## The claim

One sentence. What they want associated with their name.

Structure that usually works:

> I help [specific audience] [achieve specific outcome] by [unusual method or
> vantage point].

Use it as scaffolding, then break it — the formula shape is recognizable and
faintly generic. The finished claim should sound like a person talking.

**Strong:** "I'm a high-school physics teacher who rebuilds famous experiments
with $20 of hardware-store parts and films what actually happens."

**Weak:** "I'm an education innovator passionate about making STEM accessible
and engaging for the next generation."

The first excludes almost everyone, is checkable, and implies a story. The
second could be on fifty thousand profiles.

Prefer their own phrasing from the interview. A slightly rough sentence in
their voice outperforms a polished one in yours, because it survives contact
with how they actually talk.

## The point of view

The claim says what they do. The point of view says what they think — and it's
what makes them worth following rather than merely hiring.

A usable point of view is:

- **Arguable** — a competent peer could disagree
- **Held for a reason** — backed by something they've seen, not a vibe
- **Repeatable** — they can defend it a hundred times without getting bored

Write it as a short thesis with the reasoning attached. Two or three sentences.

If nothing arguable came out of the interview, go back to Stage D. Someone
with real experience always has an unpopular opinion; it just may take a
direct question to surface it.

## Proof

Claims need receipts. Sort what the interview produced into:

- **Credentials** — verifiable standing. Cheap to state, weak alone.
- **Artifacts** — things they made that others can inspect. Strongest form.
- **Results** — outcomes with numbers attached.
- **Scars** — hard-won specifics, including failures. Unusually persuasive
  because they're hard to fake and signal honesty.

Every major claim in the strategy should point to at least one of these. If a
claim has no proof, either find some or soften the claim — unsupported
assertion is what makes personal-brand writing feel hollow.

Note gaps honestly. "You have no public artifacts yet" is useful information
and usually converts directly into the first item on the 90-day plan.

## Naming the competition

Identify who currently owns the audience's attention. For each, note what
they're good at and what they systematically miss. The gap is the opening.

Positioning is relative. "Better" is not a position; "different in a way this
audience specifically needs" is.

## Presenting options

Bring back two or three real alternatives that make **different bets about the
audience** — the same claim narrowed three ways is not a choice.

For each: the wedge, the claim, who it wins, what it gives up, and what it
demands of them week to week.

Then say which you'd pick and why, in one or two sentences. Withholding a
recommendation isn't neutrality — they asked for a strategist. But make the
tradeoffs legible enough that they can overrule you on grounds you don't have
access to, because they know their life and you don't.
