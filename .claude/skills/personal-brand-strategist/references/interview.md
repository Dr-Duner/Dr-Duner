# The Interview

The strategy is only as good as what this stage surfaces. Budget real time
for it — 20 to 40 minutes of back-and-forth is normal and worth it.

**One question at a time.** Then follow up on the specific thing they said.

## How to run it

Open by naming what you're doing and roughly how long it takes, so they don't
feel interrogated:

> I'm going to ask you a series of questions — one at a time — to get at what's
> actually specific about your situation. Some will feel basic and some will
> feel uncomfortably narrow; the narrow ones are where the useful material is.
> Say "skip" on anything you'd rather not answer.

Then work the stages below. They're ordered so that easy factual questions
warm up toward the harder ones. You don't have to ask every question — ask
what you still don't know.

**The follow-up is where the value is.** When an answer contains a specific —
a number, a name, a moment, a frustration — go one level deeper before moving
on. Three good follow-ups beat ten fresh questions.

Watch for the **abstraction retreat**: answering a question about a specific
moment with a general principle ("I just really care about patient
experience"). That's a signal there's a real story underneath. Ask for it:
"Can you give me the last time that actually came up?"

---

## Stage A — The factual base

Quick, low-stakes, gets them talking.

- What do you actually do day to day? Walk me through a normal Tuesday.
- How long have you been doing it?
- What are you working on right now that you'd be happy to talk about publicly?
- Is there anything you're working on that you'd rather keep quiet for now?

That last one matters — it sets the boundary before anything sensitive comes up.

## Stage B — The audience

The most common failure is an audience defined so broadly it can't be reached.
Push hard here.

- Who do you most want to reach? Be more specific than a job title.
- Picture one real person in that group. Who are they? What's their week like?
- What do they already believe about your topic?
- What do they get wrong that costs them something?
- Where do they currently go for this — who are they already listening to?
- What would make them trust someone new?

If the answer stays broad, narrow it by force: "If you could only reach 200
people and it had to be the right 200, who are they?"

## Stage C — What only they know

This is the raw material for the wedge.

- What do you know from doing the work that people writing about it don't?
- What's a thing you believe that most people in your field would push back on?
- What question do people keep coming to you with?
- What have you built, fixed, published, or survived that's hard to fake?
- What's the most expensive mistake you've made, and what did it teach you?
- Is there an unusual combination in your background — two things that rarely
  show up in the same person?

That last question is often where the whole positioning lives. A practitioner
who also builds, a clinician who also codes, a lawyer who also ships product —
the intersection is defensible in a way either half alone is not.

## Stage D — The tension

Good positioning has an edge. Find it.

- What's the standard advice in your field that you think is wrong?
- What annoys you about how your field talks about itself?
- Who's getting attention for work you think is shallow? (You don't have to
  name them publicly — this is for calibration.)
- What are you unwilling to do, even though it would probably work?

Handle this material carefully. A point of view should be arguable, not
gratuitously hostile — the goal is a stance worth defending, not a fight.

## Stage E — Constraints and appetite

A strategy that ignores these gets abandoned.

- Realistically, how much time per week can you give this?
- Writing, talking, or video — which do you least hate?
- Are there things you can't say publicly? (Employer, licensing board,
  regulatory body, clients, NDA.)
- What does success look like in a year? Be concrete — a number, a role, a
  kind of email arriving in your inbox.
- Have you tried building an audience before? What happened?

The last question is diagnostic. A previous failed attempt usually reveals the
real constraint — they hated the format, the cadence broke them, or the
audience was wrong.

## Stage F — Existing surface

- Where do you currently show up online? Send me the links.
- What does your bio say right now?
- What's the last thing you published anywhere?

If they share links and web tools are available, read them. Their current
positioning — and its gap from the interview — is diagnostic.

---

## Closing the interview

Play it back before moving on. Summarize in five or six lines what you heard,
using their words, and ask what you got wrong. People correct a wrong summary
far more readily than they generate a right one from scratch, so this pass
routinely surfaces the sharpest material in the whole session.
