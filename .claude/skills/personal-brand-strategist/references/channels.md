# Channels, Formats, and Cadence

Positioning decides what to say. This decides where, how often, and in what
shape — and it's where most strategies quietly die, because they specify a
volume of work nobody was ever going to do.

## Choosing channels

Two questions, in order:

1. **Where is the audience already?** Not where the reach is — where *these
   specific people* are. A strategy aimed at hospital administrators does not
   belong on TikTok, however large TikTok is.
2. **What can they sustain?** Someone who hates being on camera will not build
   a video habit, and forcing it produces a few bad videos and a lot of guilt.

**Pick one primary channel and at most one secondary.** Spreading across four
platforms at launch produces four neglected accounts. Depth on one beats
presence on many, and the primary can feed the others later by repurposing.

## Platform notes

**LinkedIn** — Best for professional and B2B audiences, and unusually good for
niche expertise because the feed rewards specificity over polish. Text posts
outperform links. Comments on other people's posts are underrated for a cold
start: you borrow their audience while building your own.

**X / Twitter** — Fast, conversational, good for developers, founders, and
public thinking. Rewards volume and reply activity more than any other
platform. Weak for reaching most non-technical professional audiences.

**YouTube** — The highest-effort channel and the most durable. Videos keep
earning for years, unlike feed posts that die in 48 hours. Right when the topic
benefits from demonstration. Wrong as a first channel unless they already
enjoy making video.

**Newsletter** — The only channel they own. No algorithm between them and the
reader, and it survives any platform's decline. Slow to grow, so it usually
works best as the destination that a feed channel feeds into rather than the
starting point.

**Industry-specific venues** — Conferences, trade publications, professional
forums, podcasts in the niche. Often far higher signal per unit effort than
social platforms for specialized audiences, and much less crowded. Consider
these seriously before defaulting to a big platform.

**Podcast guesting** — Underrated cold start. Borrows an existing audience,
requires no ongoing cadence, and produces a durable artifact. Strong early
move for someone with a story but no following.

## Formats

Design three or four **repeatable formats** rather than a list of one-off
ideas. A format is a shape they can refill indefinitely, which is what makes
publishing sustainable — the hard part becomes noticing material, not
inventing it.

Formats that tend to work:

- **The case** — a specific situation, what was done, what happened
- **The correction** — a common belief, why it's wrong, what's true instead
- **The teardown** — examine something real in the field, name what works
- **The receipt** — show the artifact, the number, or the result
- **The question** — a genuine open problem, asked publicly

For each chosen format, write one **worked example** using the person's actual
material from the interview. A named format with a real example is executable;
a named format alone gets stared at.

## Cadence

Be conservative. The failure mode is ambition: a plan for daily posting from
someone with a demanding job collapses in week three, and the collapse
convinces them they can't do this at all.

Reasonable starting points:

- Full-time job, low appetite → **1× per week**
- Full-time job, real motivation → **2× per week**
- Part-time or deliberate push → **3–4× per week**
- Full-time creator → daily

Pick the number they'll still be hitting in month six, and say plainly that
consistency beats volume: twice a week for a year is roughly a hundred
artifacts, and almost nobody sustains that.

Build in a **batching rhythm** where possible — one writing session producing
two or three pieces is far more durable than finding time on the day.

## The 90-day plan

Concrete, dated, and front-loaded with things that can be done immediately.

- **Week 1** — Fix the surface: bio, headline, about section. One hour of work
  that every future visitor sees.
- **Weeks 2–4** — Publish the first pieces in the chosen formats. Goal is
  starting the habit, not going viral.
- **Weeks 5–8** — Add the second motion: commenting, guesting, outreach.
- **Weeks 9–12** — Review what got traction, double down, cut what didn't.

Include a **first action they can take today** — usually rewriting one bio
line. Momentum comes from finishing something small immediately.

## Measurement

Separate the two kinds of signal, because confusing them causes people to quit
during the period when things are actually working:

- **Leading** (weeks) — publishing consistency, reply quality, who engages,
  whether the right names start showing up
- **Lagging** (months) — followers, inbound, opportunities, revenue

Early on, only the leading indicators mean anything. Followers are noisy, and
the *identity* of five engaged readers matters more than the count of five
hundred passive ones. Say this explicitly in the strategy — it's the thing
that keeps people going through the quiet first months.
